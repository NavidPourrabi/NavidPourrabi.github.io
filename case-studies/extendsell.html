Extendsell — making add-ons feel like help, not a hard sell.

Extendsell gives Shopify merchants a way to sell add-ons, warranties, and bundles alongside their main products. I work on both sides of it: the merchant building an offer in the admin, and the shopper deciding whether to accept one — including the frequently-bought-together experience.

Context

A store makes much of its margin on what a shopper adds after deciding on the main product — the case with the phone, the warranty with the appliance, the second filter with the coffee machine. Extendsell exists to make those attachments easy for a merchant to set up and easy for a shopper to accept.

Two features sit inside that promise, and I work on both: add-ons, where a merchant attaches optional extras to a specific product, and frequently bought together, where the store suggests a bundle based on what shoppers actually buy in the same order.

One product, two users who want opposite things

The merchant wants the offer visible, frequent, and hard to miss. The shopper wants to not be sold to. Almost every decision on this project is a negotiation between those two, so I made the tension the review criterion rather than something to resolve once: a design ships only if it survives both readings.

  • Merchant view — can they build a sensible offer quickly, without support?
  • Shopper view — does the suggestion read as a helpful default, or as an upsell popup?

The add-on builder

The merchant side is a configuration problem disguised as a design problem. An empty form asks the merchant to already know what a good offer looks like — most don't. So the builder leads with a starting point and a live preview, and keeps the rules in plain language instead of platform syntax.

  • Live storefront preview beside the form, so nothing gets published blind.
  • Offer rules written as sentences a merchant can read back to themselves.
  • Built to sit inside the Shopify admin, so it feels native rather than bolted on.

Frequently bought together

FBT has a credibility problem: a shopper ignores a recommendation they don't believe. So the design leads with the reason. The bundle says plainly what it is, shows the combined price against the separate one, and lets a shopper drop a single item without losing the rest of the offer.

  • The bundle is one decision made of individually removable parts — not all-or-nothing.
  • Savings shown in absolute and percentage terms, since different shoppers read different framings.
  • Placed where purchase intent is highest, rather than at the bottom of the page.
  • Inherits the store's own type and colour, so it reads as part of the shop.
