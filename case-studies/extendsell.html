Extendsell — making add-ons feel like help, not a hard sell.

Extendsell gives Shopify merchants a way to sell add-ons, warranties, and bundles alongside their main products. I designed the merchant-side builder and the shopper-side frequently-bought-together experience, and owned the rollout as PM.

Context

A Shopify store makes most of its margin on the things a shopper adds after deciding on the main product — the case with the phone, the warranty with the appliance, the second filter with the coffee machine. Extendsell exists to make those attachments easy to set up and easy to accept.

Two products sat inside that promise, and I worked on both: add-ons, where a merchant attaches optional extras to a specific product, and frequently bought together (FBT), where the store suggests a bundle based on what other shoppers actually buy in the same order.

The problem

Merchants already believed in cross-sell — they just couldn't get it to work. Setting up an offer meant editing product metafields or paying a developer, so most merchants configured one bundle, saw nothing happen, and never came back. On the storefront, suggestions showed up as a generic carousel below the fold that shoppers scrolled straight past.

Research

I ran [X] interviews with merchants across [store sizes / categories], sat in on [X] support calls, and went through [X months] of tickets tagging every complaint by theme. In parallel I pulled funnel data on where merchants dropped out of setup and where shoppers ignored the widget.

The finding that reframed the project: merchants weren't asking for more configuration power. They were asking to be told what a good offer looks like. The people who succeeded were the ones who copied a competitor's bundle — everyone else stared at an empty form.

One product, two users who want opposite things

The merchant wants the offer to be visible, frequent, and hard to miss. The shopper wants to not be sold to. Every design decision on this project was a negotiation between those two, so I made the tension explicit and used it as the review criterion: an offer ships only if it survives both readings.

  • Merchant view — can they build a sensible offer in under [X] minutes, without support?
  • Shopper view — does the suggestion look like a helpful default, or like an upsell popup?

Designing the add-on builder

I replaced the empty form with a starting point. The builder opens on templates drawn from what actually converts in that merchant's category — protection plans, consumables, "complete the look" — and every template lands pre-filled and editable. Setup became: pick a shape, adjust it, preview it live, publish.

  • Live storefront preview beside the form, so the merchant never publishes blind.
  • Rules expressed in plain language ("show this with any product tagged camera") instead of metafield syntax.
  • Built on Shopify Polaris so the app felt native to the admin merchants already knew.
  • A guardrail warning when an offer would show more than [X] add-ons at once.

Frequently bought together

FBT had a credibility problem: shoppers ignore a recommendation they don't believe. So the design leads with the reason. The bundle states plainly what it is, shows the combined price against the separate one, and lets a shopper drop any single item without losing the rest of the offer.

  • Bundle presented as one decision with individually removable items — not an all-or-nothing checkbox.
  • Savings shown as an absolute amount and a percentage, because merchants' shoppers responded to different framings.
  • Placed directly under the buy button, where intent is highest, rather than at the bottom of the page.
  • Falls back to merchant-curated picks when there isn't enough order history to make an honest recommendation.
  • Inherits the store's own type and colour tokens, so it reads as part of the shop rather than a bolted-on app.

What I'd do differently

I under-invested in the empty state for stores with no order history. FBT needs data to be credible, and new stores — the ones most eager to install the app — had none. The curated fallback came later than it should have; it belonged in v1.
